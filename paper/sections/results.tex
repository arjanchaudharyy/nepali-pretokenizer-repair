\subsection{Compression}
\label{sec:compression}

Figure~\ref{fig:ksweep} sweeps the number of new merges $K$. Under the letters-only regex (\RI{}), extension saturates almost at once. For Llama-3 the first 1{,}000 merges take the premium from $2.58$ to $2.32$, and the next 63{,}000 only to $2.20$, within $0.01$ of the floor. For Qwen3, \RI{} reaches $2.19$ by $K{=}16$k and stops at $2.18$, against a floor of $2.17$. It stops early because the letters-only regex leaves few distinct Nepali pre-tokens to merge (Appendix~\ref{app:extras}). Under the repaired regex (\RII{}), additional merges keep helping. At $K{=}32$k Nepali reaches parity with English ($1.01$) on both models, and at $64$k it is cheaper than English ($0.95$), below \texttt{o200k}. At $K{=}32$k, \RII{} needs 46\% of \RI{}'s tokens for FLORES Nepali. All 28 extended tokenizers decode losslessly and reproduce the original token ids on all 1{,}012 English devtest sentences, as \S\ref{sec:floor} and Proposition~\ref{prop:lossless} predict.

Both parts of the repair are needed. Repairing the regex without new merges changes almost nothing ($2.61$ for Llama-3, $4.42$ for Qwen3), because the old merges still cut Nepali words into the old pieces. The repair also mends syllables. The share of Nepali tokens that start with a combining mark rises from 55\% (Llama-3) and 32\% (Qwen3) to 65\% and 64\% under \RI{}, since new merges can only build on mark-initial fragments, and falls to 13\% and 11\% under \RII{}.

We repeated the sweep for seven other languages written with combining marks and two controls (Table~\ref{tab:xling}, Appendix~\ref{app:xling}), learning merges on 25 to 361\,MB of FineWeb-2 text per language. At $K{=}32$k, \RII{} brings all seven to between $0.88$ and $1.24$ times English. \RI{} ends within 5\% of its floor in six of them. Thai is the exception: it is written without spaces, so its letters-only pre-tokens are long and leave \RI{} room. For Amharic and Korean, whose scripts have no combining marks, \RI{} and \RII{} are identical at every $K$, as the mechanism predicts.

\subsection{Continued pretraining}
\label{sec:cpt}

\paragraph{The registered outcome.} Table~\ref{tab:prefix} gives the results. The main hypothesis fails against \RO{}: continued pretraining without new tokens gave the lowest held-out Nepali BPB on both models. The new embeddings start as averages of existing ones, and 1\,GB of Nepali is not enough for them to catch up with tokens the base model already knows. On Qwen, \RII{} was worse than \RO{} by $0.070$, far beyond the 0.01 margin, and \RI{} by $0.060$. Against standard extension, \RII{} $-$ \RI{} $= +0.0094$ $[+0.0084, +0.0104]$: the interval excludes zero and crosses the margin, so \RII{} is worse. That verdict turns on 0.0004 BPB, which a second training run could plausibly move.

\paragraph{The BOS-consistent rerun.} In the main runs, documents were separated only by the end-of-text token, so the Llama models never saw the BOS token that the registered evaluation puts first. Under the registered prefix they give \RII{} $-$ \RI{} $= +0.0058$ $[+0.0044, +0.0073]$, which is non-inferior, but scored after the training separator, a diagnostic chosen after seeing results, the gap is $+0.027$. To resolve this we registered a third amendment before running it (Appendix~\ref{app:prereg}): the three Llama arms retrained exactly as in the main runs (same documents and order, $K{=}32{,}000$, learning rate $10^{-4}$, seed 0), except that every training document is wrapped as BOS, document, end-of-text, and scored with the registered protocol. This rerun has no prefix mismatch, and we take it as our Llama result. \RO{}, \RI{} and \RII{} reach $0.471$, $0.483$ and $0.509$. Both extension recipes are worse than \RO{}, by $+0.0124$ $[+0.0114, +0.0135]$ (\RI{}) and $+0.0380$ $[+0.0365, +0.0395]$ (\RII{}), and the repair is worse than standard extension by $+0.0256$ $[+0.0245, +0.0267]$, about 5\%. FLORES Nepali BPB orders the arms the same way ($0.607$, $0.617$, $0.626$). The three differences match the separator diagnostic of the main runs ($+0.0121$, $+0.0394$, $+0.0273$) within $0.002$, which confirms that diagnostic, and \RII{} $-$ \RI{} lies between the two earlier values, as the amendment predicted. The registered-prefix numbers of the main runs, including the non-inferior verdict, reflect the artifact.

The amendment's other prediction failed. We expected scores after BOS and after the end-of-text token to agree within $0.005$ for every arm. Instead, after the end-of-text token the rerun models are far worse ($1.017$, $0.875$ and $1.666$ for \RO{}, \RI{} and \RII{}): in the new training stream, text always follows BOS and never directly follows the end-of-text token. Together the two sets of runs support one rule: score a model after the context it was trained with.

\paragraph{What the repair buys.} \RII{} trains on 26\% fewer tokens than \RI{} for the same bytes, but this does not make it cheaper to reach a given quality: at \RII{}'s final step, the \RO{} checkpoint of the main runs is already better than the final \RII{} model on both models (Appendix~\ref{app:cptfull}). The clear gain is at inference. For the same FLORES Nepali text, \RII{} needs $2.17$ (Llama) and $2.16$ (Qwen) times fewer decoding steps than \RI{}, which has the same vocabulary size, and $2.55$ and $4.35$ times fewer than \RO{}.

\paragraph{English.} The registered English control holds. \RII{} $-$ \RO{} on held-out English is $-0.0010$ $[-0.0014, -0.0007]$ in the Llama rerun and $-0.0028$ $[-0.0032, -0.0024]$ for Qwen, both better, and \RII{} is better than \RI{} by $0.0022$ on both models. This is expected, since the tokenizer change does not touch English and every arm sees the same English data.
