\subsection{Compression}
\label{sec:compression}

Figure~\ref{fig:ksweep} sweeps the number of new merges $K$. Under the letters-only regex (\RI{}), extension saturates almost at once. For Llama-3 the first 1{,}000 merges take the premium from $2.58$ to $2.32$, and the next 63{,}000 only to $2.20$, within $0.01$ of the floor. For Qwen3, \RI{} reaches $2.19$ by $K{=}16$k and stops at $2.18$, against a floor of $2.17$. It stops early because the letters-only regex leaves few distinct Nepali pre-tokens to merge (Appendix~\ref{app:extras}). Under the repaired regex (\RII{}), additional merges keep helping. At $K{=}32$k Nepali reaches parity with English ($1.01$) on both models, and at $64$k it is cheaper than English ($0.95$), below \texttt{o200k}. At $K{=}32$k, \RII{} needs 46\% of \RI{}'s tokens for FLORES Nepali. All 28 extended tokenizers decode losslessly and reproduce the original token ids on all 1{,}012 English devtest sentences, as \S\ref{sec:floor} and Proposition~\ref{prop:lossless} predict.

Both parts of the repair are needed. Repairing the regex without new merges changes almost nothing ($2.61$ for Llama-3, $4.42$ for Qwen3), because the old merges still cut Nepali words into the old pieces. The repair also changes where tokens start. The share of Nepali tokens that start with a combining mark, a rough sign of a split syllable, rises from 55\% (Llama-3) and 32\% (Qwen3) to 65\% and 64\% under \RI{}, since new merges can only build on mark-initial fragments, and falls to 13\% and 11\% under \RII{}. The mark-initial share does not predict model quality in \S\ref{sec:cpt}: \RII{} has by far the fewest mark-initial tokens yet trails \RO{} on both models.

We repeated the sweep for seven other languages written with combining marks and two controls (Table~\ref{tab:xling}, Appendix~\ref{app:xling}), learning merges on 25 to 361\,MB of FineWeb-2 text per language. At $K{=}32$k, \RII{} brings all seven to between $0.88$ and $1.24$ times English. \RI{} ends within 5\% of its floor in six of them. Thai is the exception: it is written without spaces, so its letters-only pre-tokens are long and leave \RI{} room. For Amharic and Korean, whose scripts have no combining marks, \RI{} and \RII{} are identical at every $K$, as the mechanism predicts.

\subsection{Continued pretraining}
\label{sec:cpt}

\paragraph{The registered outcome.} Table~\ref{tab:prefix} gives the results. The main hypothesis fails against \RO{}: continued pretraining without new tokens gave the lowest held-out Nepali BPB on both models. On Qwen, \RII{} was worse than \RO{} by $0.070$, far beyond the 0.01 margin, and \RI{} was worse by $0.060$. Against standard extension, \RII{} $-$ \RI{} $= +0.0094$ $[+0.0084, +0.0104]$ (registered piece-level bootstrap: $[+0.0086, +0.0101]$), labelled ``worse'' although the point estimate lies inside the margin; averaged over two seeds it is $+0.0122$ $[+0.0112, +0.0132]$.

\paragraph{The BOS-consistent rerun.} The main Llama runs never saw BOS (\S\ref{sec:setup}). Under the registered prefix they give \RII{} $-$ \RI{} $= +0.0058$ $[+0.0043, +0.0073]$, non-inferior although it excludes zero, but $+0.027$ after the training separator. A third amendment retrained the three Llama arms with BOS before every document and nothing else changed; we take this rerun as our Llama result. \RO{}, \RI{} and \RII{} reach $0.471$, $0.483$ and $0.509$: \RI{} is worse than \RO{} by $0.0124$ $[0.0114, 0.0135]$ and \RII{} worse than \RI{} by $0.0256$ $[0.0245, 0.0267]$, about 5\% ($0.0252$ over two seeds). These match the separator diagnostic within $0.002$, so the main runs' registered-prefix numbers reflect a scoring artifact of the unseen BOS token; after the end-of-text token the rerun scores $0.875$ to $1.666$ (Appendix~\ref{app:cptfull}).


\paragraph{Matched steps.} At equal bytes, \RII{} takes 26\% fewer steps than \RI{}. A fourth amendment retrained \RII{} for \RI{}'s step count (737 for Llama with BOS, 748 for Qwen), drawing the extra 35\% of windows from a second pass over its stream, and a fifth added second seeds. Averaged over two seeds, matched-steps \RII{} is \emph{better} than \RI{} on Qwen by $0.0099$ $[0.0088, 0.0109]$ ($0.549$ against $0.559$) and worse on Llama by $0.0120$ $[0.0111, 0.0129]$ ($0.495$ against $0.483$); \RO{} stays best, by $0.049$ and $0.024$. Matching steps thus reverses the equal-bytes gap on Qwen and halves it on Llama, though with 35\% of the data repeated. Seeds move each arm by about $0.003$ or less (Appendix~\ref{app:extra-runs}).

\paragraph{Learning rate.} A fifth and a sixth amendment repeated the Qwen arms at $3{\times}10^{-4}$ instead of $10^{-4}$ (one seed, \RII{} at matched steps). Every arm improves, \RO{} by $0.021$, \RI{} by $0.052$ and \RII{} by $0.046$, but the order holds: \RO{} $0.479$, \RII{} $0.504$, \RI{} $0.509$. \RII{} beats \RI{} by $0.0057$ $[0.0047, 0.0066]$ and trails \RO{} by $0.024$ $[0.023, 0.026]$, so our predictions that \RO{} would gain little and \RII{} would match it failed. English gets worse: $0.875$ to $0.879$ BPB, against $0.862$ to $0.864$ at $10^{-4}$ and $0.873$ for the base model.

\paragraph{What the repair buys.} The clear gain is in token count. For the same FLORES Nepali text, \RII{} needs 46\% of \RI{}'s tokens on both models, and 39\% (Llama) and 23\% (Qwen) of \RO{}'s. We have not shown faster generation of equally good text: our timings forced 128 generated tokens per model on shared GPUs, used the original Llama runs and compared different outputs. In them \RII{}'s generations carry 1.55 (Llama) and 1.47 (Qwen) times as many bytes per token as \RI{}'s, and throughput is 1.48 and 1.50 times higher, which we treat as indicative only.

\paragraph{English.} The registered English control holds: \RII{} $-$ \RO{} on held-out English is $-0.0010$ $[-0.0014, -0.0007]$ (Llama rerun) and $-0.0028$ $[-0.0032, -0.0024]$ (Qwen), and \RII{} beats \RI{} by $0.0022$ on both. ``English-preserving'' refers to token ids only: every Llama arm ends worse on English than the base model ($0.798$ to $0.804$ against $0.788$).
