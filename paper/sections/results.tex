\section{Results}
\label{sec:results}

This section reports the controlled experiments: compression of the extended tokenizers and continued pretraining.

\subsection{Compression of repaired tokenizers}
\label{sec:compression}

Figure~\ref{fig:ksweep} sweeps the number of new merges $K$ for both models. Under the letters-only regex (\RI{}), extension saturates almost at once: for Llama-3 the first 1{,}000 merges take the premium from $2.58\times$ to $2.32\times$, and the next 63{,}000 only to $2.20\times$, within $0.01$ of the pre-token floor ($2.19\times$). For Qwen3, \RI{} cuts the premium from $4.42\times$ to $2.19\times$ by $K{=}16$k and then stops at $2.18\times$ against a floor of $2.17\times$. That \RI{} comes this close to its floor, rather than merely staying above it, is consistent with the small number of distinct Nepali pre-tokens the letters-only regex produces: the 1\,GB merge-training text has about 124k distinct pre-tokens under it, against 1.54M under the repaired regex. Under the repaired regex (\RII{}), the same merge budget keeps paying: $1.11\times$ at $K{=}16$k, $1.01\times$ at $32$k and $0.95\times$ at $64$k for both models, below parity with English and below \texttt{o200k}. At the pre-registered $K{=}32{,}000$, \RII{} encodes Nepali in 46\% of \RI{}'s tokens for both models.

All 28 extended tokenizers are lossless on the first 300 FLORES Nepali and English sentences (all 1{,}012 at $K{=}32{,}000$) and give token ids identical to the original tokenizer on all 1{,}012 English devtest sentences, as Proposition~\ref{prop:lossless} (for \RII{}) and its argument in \S\ref{sec:ceiling} (for \RI{}) require. FLORES dev, which we used to check the choice of $K$, gives the same picture within $0.02$.

\paragraph{Regex alone, added tokens, and syllables.} The repaired regex without new merges changes almost nothing ($2.61\times$ Llama, $4.42\times$ Qwen), and registering \RI{}'s new strings as added tokens does no better than \RI{}; the gain of \RII{} needs both the repair and new merges. \RII{} also cuts the share of Devanagari tokens that begin with a combining mark to 15\% (Llama) and 12\% (Qwen), where \RI{} raises it to 67\% (Appendix~\ref{app:extras}).

\paragraph{Other scripts.} We repeated the sweep for nine more languages, using the FineWeb-2 test split of each (25--361\,MB) to learn merges, with the same Devanagari-style output filter adapted to each script (Appendix~\ref{app:xling}). Table~\ref{tab:xling} (Appendix~\ref{app:xling}) gives the result at $K{=}32{,}000$; for example, \RII{} takes Tamil from $7.65\times$ to $1.02\times$ and Burmese from $11.62\times$ to $1.21\times$ on Llama-3, where \RI{} stops at $2.67\times$ and $3.02\times$. For every language written with combining marks except Thai, \RI{} stops at its floor and \RII{} reaches $0.88$--$1.24\times$. For the two scripts without combining marks, Amharic and Korean, \RI{} and \RII{} are identical at every $K$, as the mechanism predicts. All 108 extended tokenizers in this sweep are lossless and give English token ids identical to their base tokenizer on all 1{,}012 English devtest sentences.



\subsection{Continued pretraining}
\label{sec:cpt}


Table~\ref{tab:cpt} (Appendix~\ref{app:cptfull}) reports every run and Table~\ref{tab:prefix} the paired comparisons. Each difference is the pre-registered paired bootstrap over held-out Nepali pieces (bits per byte, 95\% interval; positive means the first arm is worse).

\paragraph{A scoring artifact, and how we report it.} The registered protocol scores every document after the model's beginning-of-sequence token, which Llama has and Qwen does not. Continued pretraining, however, never showed the models that token: documents were separated only by the end-of-text token. After seeing the first results we found that this mismatch distorts the comparison. Scores after the beginning-of-sequence token move by different amounts in different arms relative to scores after the end-of-text token: by $+0.003$ for \RO{}, $+0.051$ for \RI{} and $+0.029$ for \RII{} (Llama), and for the embedding-only warm-up of Experiment B1 the shift is large (\RO{}: 0.779 against 0.471). We attribute this to training updating that token's embedding, which in a model with tied embeddings still receives gradient as an output class although it never occurs as input; in the warm-up model its norm grew from 0.863 in the base model to 0.896. We therefore report every Llama comparison twice: under the registered prefix, and, as a diagnostic chosen after seeing results, under the end-of-text token every training document followed. We read the second as the like-for-like measure of what the tokenizer does. Qwen has no beginning-of-sequence token, so its two columns are identical.

\paragraph{Extension costs Nepali quality at this budget.} On both models and under both prefixes, continued pretraining without new tokens (\RO{}) gives the lowest held-out Nepali BPB: 0.471 for Llama-3.2-1B (from 0.766 for the base model, scored after its own beginning-of-sequence token) and 0.501 for Qwen3-0.6B (from 0.852). Adding 32{,}000 tokens costs Llama $+0.012$ bits per byte under the original regex (\RI{}) and $+0.039$ with the repair (\RII{}), and Qwen $+0.060$ and $+0.070$. The new rows start from averages of existing rows and, with 1\,GB of Nepali, do not catch up with the original vocabulary, which the base model already knows. \RO{} is also best at English-to-Nepali translation on both models.

\paragraph{The repair trades a little quality for efficiency.} Against standard extension, \RII{} is worse on held-out Nepali by $+0.027$ $[+0.027, +0.028]$ bits per byte for Llama and $+0.0094$ $[+0.0086, +0.0101]$ for Qwen (about 6\% and 2\%). Under the registered prefix the Llama gap is $+0.006$; under the rules of amendment 2, non-inferiority within 0.01 holds for Llama only under that prefix, and is not shown for Qwen. In exchange, \RII{} needs 26\% fewer training tokens than \RI{} for the same bytes, is slightly better on English for both models, and generates Nepali 1.48$\times$ (Llama) and 1.50$\times$ (Qwen) faster than \RI{} and 1.83$\times$ and 2.41$\times$ faster than \RO{}, because each token carries more text. When \RI{} is stopped after the same number of steps as \RII{} (scored on 1\,MB with the registered prefix), \RII{} is $+0.0056$ $[+0.0038, +0.0075]$ worse for Llama and tied for Qwen; \RI{}'s learning rate has not annealed at that point.

\paragraph{An embedding warm-up narrows the gap (Experiment B1).} Training only the embedding matrix first, on 10\% of the stream at a higher learning rate, and then the whole model, leaves \RO{} unchanged (0.4705 against 0.4707, training-separator prefix) and improves \RII{} from 0.510 to 0.501, shrinking \RII{}'s cost relative to \RO{} from $+0.039$ to $+0.031$ $[+0.030, +0.031]$ bits per byte (HB1, supported). %%B1R1
These are single runs at one budget; the planned 3\,GB experiment (B3) was not run, for budget reasons.

\paragraph{English is unaffected.} The registered English control holds: \RII{} minus \RO{} on held-out English is $+0.0003$ $[-0.0001, +0.0007]$ bits per byte for Llama (non-inferior) and $-0.0028$ $[-0.0031, -0.0025]$ for Qwen (better), as the invariance result predicts for the tokenizer and the equal English data in every arm predicts for the model.
